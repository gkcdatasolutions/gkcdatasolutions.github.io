About

I’m Gord Cross.

Principal, GKC Data Solutions Inc. · British Columbia, Canada

From a business conversation to working software.

I work with the people who know the business, understand the problem with them, and carry that understanding into the software.

That can mean mapping a process with operations or finance, tracing a rule through a SQL database, or writing the code that connects two applications. Moving between those parts of the work is central to how I approach a project.

Often the starting point is simply that something needs to change. I look at the process and assess existing software before recommending a way forward. If an application on the market meets the need, I recommend buying it.

My work includes enterprise integration, ERP-related data transformation, email filing workflows and local AI-assisted document discovery. The selected projects describe what those systems do.

How I got here

I came to software from forestry. I had been with Industrial Forestry Service since 1997 when, in 2000, the industry here slowed down and we were laying people off every winter, so I decided to go back to school for computer science. When I told them what I was planning, they asked whether I could already program. I could, because I had been teaching myself C++ at home, and it turned out they had an application written in C for cruise compilations, the timber-volume calculations the industry runs on. I stayed, and wrote software there until 2003. My first programming job ran on a VAX under VMS, through a TN-3270 terminal.

The interest was not new. I had been running Unix boxes at home since I was young, setting up firewalls and playing with databases, right through my forestry years.

I hold three degrees: forestry from the University of New Brunswick, biology from Bishop’s University, and computer science from the University of Northern British Columbia. The first two are why I am at home in resource-based and professional service organizations. I have spent time inside how they operate, not only inside their software.

From October 2003 I spent twenty-two years at Allnorth Consultants, until I resigned in April 2026 to run GKC full time. I rewrote their ERP and we ran it, dual currency, for about eighteen years. Most of my work was shared services: the project management office, finance, payroll and HR. Onboarding and offboarding, vendors, accounts receivable and collections, including a collections module I rewrote outright; and for the PMO, resource scheduling, revenue forecasting, variance reporting and budget tracking.

We eventually replaced my ERP with a commercial one, and I ran that migration. The hard part was never the ETL. The two systems recognised revenue differently: mine recognised it as billed, the new one tested whether the fee type and the project budget had room for the effort accruing against it. Variance, write-offs and revenue all had to be rethought. It took six to nine months, with every step scripted in SQL so that the cutover was a set of buttons we could press over a single weekend.

Allnorth also sold software alongside its engineering services: inspection databases for roads, bridges, and concrete in plants and facilities. One client was Northern Development Initiative Trust, which takes government funding and, through public-private partnerships, makes grants and loans to communities and small businesses developing the northern economy. We built their first ERP. When the firm grew and chose to stop selling software externally, putting its development into shared services instead, they agreed I should keep that client rather than leave them unsupported. GKC started in 2012 for that reason.

I rebuilt the Trust’s ERP in WPF, moving off WinForms before it became a dead end, and learning WPF and MVVM properly rather than carrying old habits across. It took more than a year alongside the day job, and grew web components that integrated with their website. It ran until 2025.

In 2025 the Trust moved to Salesforce, recommended by another trust that had found it carried programs built for grant funding. I was not supposed to be part of that work. The vendor configuring the new system saw how much the application it was replacing actually did, and I was kept on for a further year to carry the data across.

Where I am most useful: moving data out of an old system into a new one without losing its meaning, and building the small tool that retires a spreadsheet, a desktop application or website with the controls that keep data clean and stop the same thing being typed twice.

What matters in the work

GKC is a Canadian consulting and software company. Business analysis and hands-on development are part of the same service.

Put the client’s interests first
I recommend software because it fits the client’s needs. Assessing existing products comes before proposing custom development. The opportunity to write new software is never a reason to choose it.
Understand the exception
The routine path is only part of the process. I want to know what happens when a record is incomplete, a delivery fails or someone has to make a judgement call.
Make decisions traceable
Field mappings and validation rules need to be understandable to the people responsible for them. With AI, that includes access to source evidence and human review.
Keep responsibility clear
Automation needs a clear point of human responsibility. Someone must be able to review an exception and decide what happens next.
Keep the system supportable
I prefer the simplest architecture that meets the requirement. Documentation and diagnostic tools are part of leaving something another person can understand and maintain.
